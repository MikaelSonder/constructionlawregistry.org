Executive Summary

Construction law in England and Wales is the legal and operational framework governing land development, planning, design, building control, procurement, construction, completion, occupation and use of building and infrastructure projects. It combines common-law contract principles with statute, planning and building-regulations systems, health and safety law, public procurement, environmental regulation, payment and adjudication rights, professional standards and project-specific contractual documentation.

England and Wales share much of the core construction-contract and health-and-safety framework, including the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996, the Scheme for Construction Contracts, the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 and the Procurement Act 2023. Planning and building-control administration is, however, local and devolved in important respects. England and Wales have distinct planning policy, building-regulations guidance, development-plan and building-safety arrangements. The project location and competent local planning and building-control authority must therefore be identified at inception.

Planning permission and building-regulations approval are separate workstreams. Planning addresses the acceptability of development and land use; building regulations address technical standards for building work. In England, Approved Documents provide statutory guidance on practical ways to comply with the Building Regulations 2010. In Wales, Welsh Government administers Welsh building-regulation policy and Approved Documents, while local planning authorities administer planning applications and development-plan controls.

Contract and payment law is a defining feature of the jurisdiction. Subject to statutory scope and exclusions, the Construction Act establishes rights to adjudication, interim or stage payment, payment notices, suspension for non-payment and restrictions on pay-when-paid clauses. CDM 2015 imposes duties on clients, designers, principal designers, principal contractors, contractors and workers. England additionally has the Building Safety Act 2022 regime for higher-risk buildings; the Building Safety Regulator is the building-control authority for higher-risk building work in England, with gateway controls across design and construction.

Object Definition

Construction law is the professional legal function concerned with establishing and managing the legal framework for a construction project. In England and Wales it includes planning, building regulations, building control, construction contracts, professional appointments, procurement, CDM health and safety, environmental approvals, payment, adjudication, claims and dispute resolution.

Purpose and Primary Outcome

The function exists to make project responsibilities, approvals, contractual risk allocation and compliance duties understandable and workable before disputes or regulatory failures arise. A sound construction-law framework enables parties to determine who must perform, under which requirements, on what timetable and with what consequence if performance changes or fails.

PurposeTo organise construction activity within applicable England and Wales planning, building-regulations, contractual, safety, procurement, environmental and payment frameworks.
Primary OutcomeA legally coherent project structure: appropriate planning and building-control approvals, documented dutyholders, workable contracts, compliance records and a defined route for change, delay, payment, adjudication and dispute issues.

Construction-law work is normally activated by a defined project event rather than by a single abstract legal question. The work often begins before land acquisition, planning strategy, consultant appointment or tender issue and continues through construction, building-control completion, occupation, defects management and any claim process.

Jurisdiction Characteristics

England and Wales are closely connected common-law jurisdictions but should not be treated as a single uniform administrative system. Construction contracts, adjudication and CDM duties are broadly shared. Planning and building-control administration operate through local planning authorities and building-control bodies, with policy and procedural differences arising from devolution. England has the additional Building Safety Regulator regime for higher-risk buildings.

Institutional StructureUK-wide legislation interacts with England- and Wales-specific policy, local planning authorities, building-control bodies, environmental regulators, safety regulators, utilities, public contracting authorities and courts.
Planning SystemPlanning permission is generally administered by the relevant local planning authority. Development plans, national policy, local validation requirements, environmental assessment, conditions and appeal rights shape the planning route.
Building RegulationsBuilding regulations are distinct from planning. In England, Approved Documents provide statutory guidance on how to meet the Building Regulations 2010; Wales issues its own building-regulation policy materials and Approved Documents.
Building Safety — EnglandFor higher-risk building work in England, the Building Safety Regulator is the building-control authority. Higher-risk building definitions and gateway procedures require project-specific assessment.
Language ExpectationEnglish is central to legislation, planning, contracts, procurement, regulatory records and court proceedings. Welsh has official-language status in Wales and may be used in public-sector and local-authority contexts.

Process Flow and Decision Tree

A project should be assessed as a sequence of linked legal decisions. The order varies by whether the project is in England or Wales, its site, building type, planning setting, environmental context and delivery model, but late treatment of planning, building regulations, CDM, procurement, payment or contract documents can create substantial execution risk.

1. Jurisdiction, Site and Development ReviewConfirm England or Wales, local planning authority, building-control route, property rights, planning designation, local development plan, heritage, flood, ecology, utilities, highways and anticipated approval route.
2. Planning and Regulatory MappingDetermine planning permission, development consent, listed-building, environmental, flood-risk, drainage, highways, utility, fire, building-regulations, Building Safety Regulator and specialist approval requirements.
3. Design, Dutyholders and Technical DocumentationAppoint competent designers and, where required, principal designer and principal contractor; prepare planning documents, building-regulation information, specifications, risk controls and compliance evidence.
4. Procurement and Contract StructureSelect traditional, design and build, management contracting, construction management, EPC, framework or other delivery model; assess Procurement Act 2023 requirements where relevant; select and amend the contract form.
5. Approval, Mobilisation and Project ControlsObtain permissions and building-control approvals; establish CDM appointments, construction phase plan, insurance, bonds, warranties, programme, notices, payment procedures, quality and site controls.
6. Construction and AdministrationManage building-control inspections, changes, CDM duties, design risk, notices, payment notices, valuations, delay, extensions of time, compensation events, defects, quality and project records.
7. Completion and Close-OutManage completion, building-control completion certification, commissioning, health and safety file, as-builts, warranties, final account, defects period, occupation requirements and unresolved claims.
Decision sequence: Is the project in England or Wales? → Which local planning and building-control authority applies? → Is planning permission required? → What building-regulation route applies? → Does CDM require principal dutyholders or F10 notification? → Is it a higher-risk building in England? → Is the project a covered public procurement? → What statutory payment and adjudication rights apply?

Cross-Border Relevance

Foreign companies frequently participate in England and Wales projects as developers, contractors, consultants, suppliers, investors, funders, insurers or group entities. Cross-border projects require a clear connection between local planning, building-control, safety, contract and procurement requirements and the corporate, tax, immigration, insurance, financing and supply-chain arrangements used by project participants.

RecognitionEngland and Wales are internationally recognised construction and dispute-resolution jurisdictions, but domestic planning, building regulations, CDM, statutory payment and adjudication rights, procurement rules and local authority practice remain central to work performed there.
Foreign CompaniesForeign participants should assess entity establishment, tax, immigration, professional and contractor competence, insurance, collateral warranties, bonds, local authority requirements, procurement eligibility and contracting authority before mobilisation.
Language ConsiderationsEnglish is central to contracts, planning, building control, procurement, statutory interpretation and court proceedings. Welsh-language requirements and public-sector practice can be relevant in Wales.
International RulesInternational investment, sanctions, trade, customs, imported products, product safety, technical standards, financing, international arbitration and supply-chain requirements may affect projects in addition to domestic construction law.
Typical RisksAssuming planning permission resolves building-regulations requirements, failing to distinguish England from Wales, overlooking CDM dutyholder appointments, non-compliant Construction Act payment terms, or applying international contract forms without adapting them to mandatory statutory rights and local approvals.

Operating Constraints, Risks and Costs

Planning and Building-Control RiskPlanning permission and building-regulations approval are separate processes. Local validation requirements, conditions, code compliance, inspections, completion certification and appeal routes can materially affect programme and cost.
England–Wales Regulatory RiskCore contract and CDM law are broadly shared, but planning policy, building-regulations guidance, local procedures and building-safety arrangements differ. The correct England or Wales regulatory route must be determined early.
CDM and Safety RiskCDM 2015 assigns duties to clients, designers, principal designers, principal contractors and contractors. Projects with more than one contractor require principal dutyholders and a construction phase plan; notifiable projects require an F10 notification to HSE.
Higher-Risk Building RiskIn England, higher-risk building work can fall under Building Safety Regulator building-control jurisdiction and gateway requirements. The applicability analysis should precede design freeze, procurement and mobilisation.
Payment and Adjudication RiskThe Construction Act imposes mandatory payment and adjudication rights. Defective payment-notice mechanisms, pay less notices, suspension rights, adjudication deadlines and insolvency clauses can materially affect cash flow and dispute exposure.
Public Procurement RiskCovered public procurement is regulated by the Procurement Act 2023, which governs award, entry into and management of public contracts. Contracting authority, threshold, exemption, procedure, transparency and remedies analysis is project-specific.
Cost DriversLegal and professional costs are influenced by planning complexity, building-control route, higher-risk status, environmental approvals, delivery model, public procurement, contract form, payment and claims administration, programme risk and dispute forum.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is construction law the same in England and Wales?Many core rules are shared, including the Construction Act, CDM 2015 and much of the common-law contract framework. Planning policy, building-regulations guidance, local procedures and certain building-safety arrangements differ, so the project location must be identified early.
Are planning permission and building regulations the same thing?No. Planning permission concerns whether development is acceptable in land-use and policy terms. Building regulations concern technical standards for building work. A project can require both approvals, and one does not replace the other.
What is statutory adjudication?Subject to scope and exclusions, a party to a construction contract has the right to refer a dispute to adjudication at any time. The statutory procedure aims at appointment and referral within seven days and a decision within 28 days of referral, subject to permitted extensions.
What payment rights does the Construction Act provide?The Act provides statutory rights to interim, stage or periodic payments for most construction contracts lasting more than 45 days, requires an adequate payment mechanism and final payment date, regulates payment and pay less notices, permits suspension for non-payment and restricts conditional payment provisions.
What does CDM 2015 require?CDM 2015 applies broadly to construction work. Clients must ensure projects are suitably managed; designers must eliminate or control foreseeable risks; principal designers and principal contractors coordinate relevant phases where more than one contractor is involved; and contractors must plan, manage and monitor work under their control.
What is a higher-risk building in England?For the Building Safety Act occupied-building regime, a higher-risk building in England is generally at least 18 metres or seven storeys and contains at least two residential units. The design and construction regime has related but distinct definitions and exclusions; project-specific advice is required.
